Is It Too Late to Become a Barber? Career Change at 30, 40 or 50

From the American Barber Institute blog.

No, it's not too late to become a barber, whether you're 30, 40, 50 or older. New York sets a minimum age to become a barber (17), but no maximum. Training is short compared with most career changes: a 500-hour program takes about four months on a weekday schedule or six to seven months on weekends, and you can keep working while you train. What matters isn't your age. It's whether you're willing to put in the hours, practice and build a clientele.

Is there an age limit to become a barber in New York?

There is a minimum age, not a maximum. To get a New York barber license you need to be at least 17, complete a barber course at a New York State-approved school, complete the state-approved infection control and contagious diseases course, pass a physical exam certified by a physician, physician's assistant or nurse practitioner, and pass the State practical exam. Nothing in the requirements depends on how old you are once you're past 17.

The same is true for barber school admission. At American Barber Institute, you need to be at least 17 (under 18 needs parental permission) and have a high school diploma or GED, or pass the Ability-To-Benefit entrance exam at the school. If you finished school a long time ago and can't find your diploma, or never finished, that's not necessarily a dead end. Our guide on going to barber school without a high school diploma explains the ATB option.

How long will it take to change careers?

Much less time than going back to college. ABI's 500-Hour Master Barber Program runs on three schedules:

  • Morning: Mon–Fri, 8 AM–2 PM, about 17 weeks.
  • Afternoon: Mon–Fri, 2 PM–8 PM, about 17 weeks.
  • Weekend: Sat–Sun, 9 AM–7 PM, about 27 weeks.

After you graduate, you apply for your license and take the State practical exam. Compared with a degree program, it is a short, clearly defined path into a new trade.

Can I keep my job while I train?

For most career-changers this is the real question, because rent, family and bills don't stop. The schedules are built with working adults in mind. The weekend class leaves Monday to Friday free for a full-time job, and the afternoon class can work if your job ends by early afternoon. The trade-off is time: the weekend route takes longer to finish, but you keep your full income the whole way through. For a closer look, see going to barber school while working full-time.

Paying for school as an adult

Tuition at ABI is $3,600 (afternoon), $4,600 (weekend) or $5,600 (morning), including the $100 non-refundable registration fee. Weekly payment plans start from $160 a week, from $200 down, so you can pay as you go out of your current paycheck. Tools, books and supplies are extra.

Some adults also qualify for help:

  • ACCES-VR, New York State's vocational rehabilitation program, can cover tuition, fees, books and tools for eligible New Yorkers with a documented disability.
  • VA education benefits, including the Post-9/11 GI Bill and VR&E, are accepted at ABI's Manhattan campus only. Many veterans start a second career after service, and barbering is a good fit for that kind of discipline.

What can I realistically earn?

Earnings in barbering vary a lot depending on your skill, your speed, where you work, how many clients you build and whether you rent a booth or own a shop. As rough estimates only, a barber's first year often lands around $35,000–$45,000, years two to three around $50,000–$70,000, and experienced booth renters or shop owners can reach $75,000–$100,000 or more. These numbers aren't guaranteed, and many barbers earn more or less. Building a loyal clientele takes time at any age. For more context, read how much money barbers make.

Strengths older career-changers bring to the chair

Starting later can feel intimidating, especially in a class with younger students. But adults who change careers often bring real advantages:

  • Work ethic and reliability. Years of showing up for work translate directly into showing up for school, and later, for clients.
  • People skills. Barbering is as much about conversation and trust as it is about cutting hair. If you've worked in customer service, sales, hospitality, healthcare or management, you already know how to read people and make them comfortable.
  • An existing network. Coworkers, neighbors, church members, friends and family can become your first clients. A clientele is built one person at a time, and adults often know a lot of people.
  • Business sense. If you've managed money, people or a budget, you're ahead when it comes to renting a booth or eventually opening a shop. (A separate Barber Shop license is required to own or operate a shop in New York.)
  • Patience and focus. Adults often know why they're there and what they want. That clarity helps when you're working through hundreds of hours of practice.
  • Relating to a wide range of clients. Many clients are adults themselves and appreciate a barber who understands their lifestyle, profession and style.

Things to think about before you start

Barbering is physical work. You'll be on your feet for long stretches and using your hands and wrists constantly, so think honestly about your stamina. Your first year is often about building speed and clientele rather than maximum income. And you'll be learning something brand new, which takes humility. None of these are reasons to stop. They're reasons to plan.

The bottom line

Your age isn't the obstacle. Finding a schedule that fits your life is, and there's one built for working adults.
